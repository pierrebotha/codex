The war ground on, a brutal rhythm of airstrikes and interceptions, but for Pieter and Ruiz, the conflict had narrowed to a single, obsessive point. The radio chatter of routine patrols masked a silent, desperate search. Every time Pieter scanned the horizon, he wasn't looking for formations; he was looking for the Black Eagle.

'You're pushing it, Pieter,' Visser warned him one evening, watching Pieter trace flight paths on the map. 'You’re burning fuel chasing shadows.'

'He's out there,' Pieter replied, his voice tight. 'He's looking for me, too.'

It was true. Across the border, Ruiz routinely altered his patrol routes, deviating from standard Soviet doctrine, scanning the lower altitudes where the Mirage preferred to hunt. The broader strategic goals of the campaign—supporting ground troops, protecting supply lines—began to feel secondary.

They encountered each other in fleeting moments. A momentary radar lock before one dove into the radar clutter of the terrain. A distant glint of silver banking sharply into the clouds. The engagements were brief, savage, and inconclusive. They were circling each other, testing boundaries, learning every nuance of the other's flying style.

The other pilots noticed. They spoke of the rivalry in hushed tones in the mess tents. It was no longer just SAAF versus FAPA. It was the Springbok and the Eagle, locked in a private war high above the burning bush, a lethal dance where only one man would eventually walk away. The sky felt stretched taut, ready to snap.

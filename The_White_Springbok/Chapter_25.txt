The mess hall was unnervingly quiet when Pieter walked in a week later. The bruising on his face had faded to a dull yellow, but the exhaustion was etched permanently into the lines around his eyes.

Conversations stopped. Pilots turned in their chairs.

Coetzee stood up, walking over slowly. 'They said you brought the Cuban back. The Black Eagle.'

'I did,' Pieter said quietly, walking to the coffee urn.

'Why?' A younger pilot asked from the back. 'He was trying to kill you.'

Pieter poured the coffee, the dark liquid swirling in the chipped mug. He thought of the fire, the minefield, the vast, indifferent emptiness of the bush. He thought of two men, stripped of their aircraft and their ideologies, relying on each other to survive.

'Because out there,' Pieter said, turning to face them, 'it doesn't matter what flag you fly. We were just two men.'

He took his coffee and walked out onto the tarmac. The ground crews were prepping a fresh Mirage F1. Beneath the cockpit canopy, the white springbok was brightly painted, pristine against the camouflage.

The story had already begun to spread. The White Springbok wasn't a legend because he had shot down the Black Eagle. The kill wasn't confirmed. The aircraft had collided. It was a draw.

He was a legend because he had done something far more difficult. He had bested the enemy, saved his life, and returned home with his humanity intact.

Pieter ran a hand over the painted springbok. The war would continue. There would be more sorties, more danger, more blood spilled on the red earth of Angola. But as he looked up at the bruised sky, he knew he was ready for whatever came next. He had faced the best the enemy had to offer, and he had survived.
